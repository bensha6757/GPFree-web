Technology

A trained expert, generated in one pass and checked before it works.

Pelora's engine takes labeled examples of a task and emits a trained expert for it in a single pass, in about 13 seconds. There is no training run to schedule.

01

Generation, not training.

Conventional specialization means assembling a dataset, configuring a run, holding accelerators for hours, and evaluating whatever comes out. The engine replaces that loop entirely.

It learns from your labeled examples - the inputs and the outputs you actually want. It does not work from a written description of the task.

02

The verification gate.

A generated expert is not trusted because it was generated. Every candidate passes a selection gate that measures it against held-out performance on the same task, and only a candidate that clears the gate is cached and served.

This is the closed loop that makes the approach usable in production rather than interesting in a paper. An expert reaches your work only after it has been measured, so specialization is something you can verify rather than something you hope for.

03

Experts that stay current.

A fine-tuned model is a snapshot of a dataset on the day it was trained. It decays quietly while everyone assumes it is fine.

When generation costs seconds, the calculus inverts. An expert is regenerated when its data drifts, when a policy changes, or when you move to a newer base model. Base-model migration stops being a retraining program and becomes a regeneration pass over the expert cache.

The engine also keeps improving with every task it learns from.

04

Inside your perimeter.

Experts are generated from your data and are yours. For organizations that cannot send data out - banks, security, government - generation and serving run on your own GPUs, inside your own network.

The deployment story is the same one as the ownership story. Your data trains your experts, the weights belong to you, and nothing has to leave.
